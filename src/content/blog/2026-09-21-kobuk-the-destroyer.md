<p><strong>Book club: Kobuk the Destroyer: And Other Tales from the Wild, Unseen World of Test Engineering, by Alex Davies</strong></p><p>Kobuk was a bear with a useful talent for opening supposedly bear-resistant containers. Given a container full of food, he would bite, claw and generally investigate until he found a weakness. He became known as Kobuk the Destroyer. He was unlikely to be impressed by a passing test report.</p><p>Mechanical tests could assess a container’s resistance to impact or puncture. Kobuk brought something harder to reproduce: a determined user willing to try things the designer had not anticipated.</p><p>That is a useful introduction to test engineering: <strong>designing tests and experiments to understand how something behaves, where its limits are and what happens when it fails.</strong> It requires judgement about what to investigate and what the evidence actually tells us.</p><p>Davies’s book explores that work through physical products and infrastructure. I think the same approach has a much wider application. We can all use it when making engineering decisions, even when we will not be the person doing the testing.</p><figure>
  <img src="../../assets/kobuk-bear.webp" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="A grizzly bear investigating a sealed food canister">
  <figcaption>A test can reproduce a force without reproducing the behaviour behind it.</figcaption>
</figure><h2>What have we assumed?</h2><p>Every test simplifies reality. We use mocks, sample data and scripted journeys because we need controlled, repeatable conditions. The important question is what those conditions leave out.</p><p>A dependency might complete its work but lose the response. A user might repeat an action because the screen appears frozen. Someone might find a way to exploit behaviour we considered harmless.</p><p>Thinking like a test engineer means considering those possibilities while we design. How else could someone use this? What could go wrong as a result? Could we prevent the problem or make recovery easier?</p><p>Often, the user is simply trying to get something done. We should design with that in mind.</p><h2>When every component passes</h2><p>Consider a hypothetical journey between Retail and Supply Chain. A store colleague submits a customer order. Supply Chain accepts it for manufacture, but the acknowledgement does not reach Retail before the request times out.</p><p>The screen offers “Try again”. The colleague clicks it, and a second order is created.</p><p>Both teams’ checks are green. Retail handles the timeout and offers a retry. Supply Chain turns valid requests into orders. The customer still gets the wrong outcome.</p><p>The assumption between the systems is the problem. No acknowledgement does not mean no order exists. It means Retail does not yet know the outcome.</p><p>That distinction should influence the design. We need to agree how repeated submissions are recognised and how the colleague can check whether an order already exists. Those decisions cross a team boundary, so our evidence needs to cover that boundary too.</p><h2>What happens after failure?</h2><p>The duplicate order also raises a broader question: once something has gone wrong, does our response contain the damage or add to it? Physical safety engineering offers a useful way to think about this.</p><p>Hugh DeHaven’s work on crash safety drew attention to the “second collision”: after a vehicle hits something, the occupant may hit the vehicle’s interior. Preventing the crash matters, but so does designing what happens to the person when a crash occurs.</p><figure>
  <img src="../../assets/kobuk-crash-test.webp" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="A restrained crash-test dummy with an airbag in a car cabin">
  <figcaption>The response to failure determines how far its consequences travel.</figcaption>
</figure><p>In our order example, the timeout is the initial failure; duplicate manufacture is the “second collision”. The retry is intended to help, yet it turns uncertainty into a new problem for the customer. Recovery behaviour deserves the same scrutiny as the original request.</p><p>Following that chain helps us decide what to investigate. Could retries overload a struggling dependency? Could we repeat an irreversible action? How would someone establish what actually happened?</p><p>We can investigate this before release. Accept the order, interrupt the response and repeat the submission in a controlled environment. Then check both systems and the colleague’s experience. Has the customer ended up with one correct order, and can the colleague establish its status?</p><p>That gives us much more useful evidence than confirming that we caught an exception.</p><h2>The system includes its surroundings</h2><p>Following the consequences of a failure takes us beyond a single component. The same is true of successful changes: improving the property we set out to improve can reveal an interaction we had not considered.</p><p>Changes to the Golden Gate Bridge’s west-side railing helped improve its performance in high winds. They also produced an unexpected consequence: under particular wind conditions, the bridge made a striking humming sound.</p><figure>
  <img src="../../assets/kobuk-bridge.webp" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="The Golden Gate Bridge with musical notes suggesting wind-induced sound">
  <figcaption>A change can improve one property while creating a problem elsewhere.</figcaption>
</figure><p>The <a href="https://www.goldengate.org/district/district-projects/suicide-deterrent-net/bridge-sounds-during-high-wind-events/">Bridge District describes</a> measuring the sound, reproducing it in a wind tunnel and testing modifications that preserved the aerodynamic benefit. The aerodynamic improvement was real, but it did not tell the whole story: structural performance and acoustic behaviour needed different evidence.</p><p>Software has its own version of this problem. Its behaviour depends on the workloads, people and other systems around it. A configuration that works well for one workload may cause problems for another. Data can be technically valid but misleading in the context where someone uses it.</p><p>Understanding those interactions means looking beyond our own component and involving people who see parts of the system we do not. It also means learning from behaviour in production and using those observations to improve our designs and tests.</p><h2>Thinking like a test engineer</h2><p>As AI makes implementations easier to produce, I suspect this judgement will become more valuable. We risk generating changes faster than we can understand and validate them. We still need to decide what should happen and whether the evidence supports our confidence.</p><p>For the next design conversation, I would start with three questions:</p><ul><li><strong>What are we assuming?</strong></li><li><strong>How could we be wrong?</strong></li><li><strong>How would we know?</strong></li></ul><p>Pick an assumption that matters, particularly one that crosses a team boundary. Use your experience and network to get it addressed. You do not need to be responsible for executing the tests to take responsibility for the outcome.
</p>
