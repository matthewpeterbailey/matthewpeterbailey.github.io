At this year’s Ai4 2025 conference in Las Vegas, a US based AI company called Reflekta debuted what they call a ‘Soul Tech’ platform. The idea? To preserve the voices, mannerisms and stories of loved ones, allowing families to continue speaking with them long after they’ve passed away.

It sounds like science fiction and, depending on your perspective, either a touching way to hold onto memories or something straight from a cautionary episode of Black Mirror.

On launch, Reflekta shared their guiding promise:

> “At Reflekta, we believe that ownership of an AI-generated likeness remains with the individual it represents—or, after their passing, with their designated next of kin or trusted guardian. We do not claim ownership over any likeness, voice model, or memory data. Our role is to steward that data responsibly, never to possess it. Everything created within Reflekta—text, audio, video, and AI-generated interactions—exists under a framework of user control, consent, and respect. None of the artifacts or Elder likenesses created on Reflekta are accessible to anyone except the family and those they choose to share them with. Additionally, families retain the right to manage, preserve, or remove an Elder’s digital presence at any time.”

On the face of it, that’s a strong reassurance. The language of stewardship, ownership, and consent is deliberately careful, especially in an relatively unregulated industry often accused of treating personal data as fair game.

But what’s just as striking is their terminology. They don’t call their recreations “avatars” or “simulations.” They have dubbed them as Elders, a word that carries some weight - suggesting wisdom, guidance, trust - all qualities that we’re taught to respect.

This is where things get ethically complicated. When an AI speaks in the voice of a loved one, particularly one positioned as an “Elder”, when is it no longer just a tool? At what point does it become a figure of authority that we instinctively deem a credible source of information and guidance?

This raises some uncomfortable scenarios:

- Advice with consequences: what if a grieving son asks their “Elder” for career advice, and the AI produces something misleading or harmful?
- Legal and ethical responsibility: if someone commits a crime after acting on an “Elder’s” suggestion, who is accountable? The individual? The company? The AI model?
- Bad actors: could this technology be manipulated to impersonate trusted voices and spread disinformation? (It almost certainly already is - in a similar vein to deep fakes).

When you label a simulation an Elder, you’re not just preserving memory, you’re granting it influence. And unlike human mentors, AI-generated Elders can’t (currently) take responsibility for the guidance they give.

Undoubtably this is all a remarkable leap forward, but also a deeply unsettling one. Where does memory end and simulation begin? Does talking to a digital version of someone help the grieving process, or does it trap us in it? These are not just technical questions but moral ones, and I think as a society we’re still catching up.

In my role, we’re already exploring how AI can support clinicians, improve accessibility, and personalise customer experiences.

Imagine a future where a customer interacts with a virtual assistant that doesn’t just answer questions, but remembers their preferences, mimics the tone of a trusted advisor, and offers guidance that feels personal. Or a clinical support tool that uses voice synthesis to deliver care instructions in the voice of a familiar practitioner, helping patients feel more at ease.

These aren’t necessarily far-off concepts. The same underlying technologies: voice cloning, memory graphs, emotionally intelligent dialogue systems; could be adapted to create more empathetic, human-like interactions. But as the “Elders” show, the moment an AI feels familiar, perhaps the stakes change. Trust deepens and with that comes a responsibility to ensure these systems are not just technically accurate, but ethically sound.

We’ll likely need to ask hard questions over the coming years:

- How do we ensure AI advice is clinically safe and contextually appropriate?
- Who is accountable when a system gives guidance that’s followed but flawed?
- How do we design AI that supports human decision-making without replacing it?

Reflekta’s debut is a reminder that AI isn’t just changing how we work - it’s beginning to change how we remember, grieve, and seek guidance. Which is as extraordinary as it is unsettling. AI is advancing at breakneck speed, it’s the Printing Press, Personal Computer or Internet of this generation, and ‘Soul Tech’ is a striking reminder that not all of its applications will sit comfortably. While some uses - medical diagnostics, accessibility tools, faster coding assistants - feel like unambiguous progress, others tiptoe into territory that is profoundly human and deeply personal.

Whether ‘Soul Tech’ becomes a widely accepted part of life will depend not just on clever engineering, but on careful guardrails. As Engineers, we’re not just building systems, we’re shaping real world experiences. When those AI driven experiences touch on reliability, trust and care in our Supply Chain, Retail and Clinical environments, we need to be cognisant that the margin for error narrows.
